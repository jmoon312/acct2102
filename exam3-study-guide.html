How to Use This Guide

Exam 3 covers three chapters: budgeting (Ch 9), standard costs, variances, and responsibility centers (Ch 10), and capital budgeting (Ch 12). Work top-to-bottom: read each concept section, keep the Formula Quick Reference open beside you, then close the loop with the Self-Check questions and the interactive activities. Most of the exam's computational questions randomize their numbers, so drill the procedure, not a memorized answer.

Biggest trap on this exam: using the wrong volume or the wrong "base." In budgeting, keep sales units separate from production units. In variances, always compare against the standard allowed for actual output (not the original budget). In capital budgeting, use the annuity factor for equal cash flows and the PV-of-$1 factor for uneven ones.
Chapter 9

Budgeting

ApproachHow it builds the budget
IncrementalStart from the prior period's budget and adjust up/down. Fast, but can carry forward past inefficiencies.
Zero-basedStart from zero; every dollar must be justified each period. Thorough but time-consuming.
Output/inputUsed when there's a clear, measurable link between inputs and outputs (e.g., pounds per unit). Common in manufacturing, merchandising, distribution.
Activity-basedBudget the cost of each activity (setups, inspections) based on expected activity volume.
  • Budgetary slack: intentionally understating budgeted revenue or overstating budgeted expenses to make targets easier to hit. It's encouraged when pay/bonuses are tied too tightly to beating the budget.
  • Using budgets well: encourage participation at all levels, use budgets to guide behavior toward goals, and use performance reports to spot both strong and weak performance — not to punish or to bolt raises directly onto hitting the number.
Risk management steps: Identify → Assess (likelihood & impact) → Respond (avoid, transfer, mitigate, or accept) → Monitor. You manage risk — you never "eliminate every risk."
  • The sales budget drives everything — every downstream budget depends on budgeted sales.
  • Order for a manufacturer: sales → production → direct materials, direct labor, and overhead → then the budgeted income statement and cash budget.
  • For a merchandiser there's no production step; the purchases budget takes its place.
The one relationship behind all of them
Needed = What you must supply + Desired ending balance − Beginning balance
  • Production budget (manufacturer, in units): Budgeted sales + desired ending finished goods − beginning finished goods. Desired ending FG is usually a % of next period's sales.
  • Purchases budget (merchandiser): Budgeted cost of goods sold (or unit sales) + desired ending inventory − beginning inventory. Works in units or dollars.
  • Direct materials budget: materials needed for production = (production units × input per unit); then purchases = needed + desired ending materials − beginning materials.
  • Direct labor budget: total hours = (production × hours per unit) plus any fixed per-batch/per-run/per-setup hours; multiply total hours by the wage rate. Watch for a per-unit and a per-batch component in the same problem.
  • Budgeted COGS (merchandiser): often a stated % of budgeted sales.
Don't get baited by inventory of the wrong thing. "Materials used in production = production × input per unit." Beginning/ending materials inventory changes the purchases figure, not the amount used.

Chapter 10

Standard Costs, Variances & Responsibility Centers

  • Standard cost: what it should cost to produce one unit under planned operating conditions. Built from engineering analysis plus history adjusted for expected changes — not just average past costs.
Responsibility centerManager is accountable for…
Cost centerCosts only (e.g., accounting department, a factory maintenance shop).
Revenue centerRevenues only (e.g., a regional sales office).
Profit centerBoth revenues and costs — i.e., profit (e.g., an individual store).
Investment centerRevenues, costs, and the assets invested; evaluated on ROI (e.g., an autonomous division).
  • A flexible budget restates the budget at the actual output level: standard cost per unit × actual units produced. Fixed costs stay fixed.
  • A flexible-budget variance compares actual cost to the flexible budget. Actual > standard → Unfavorable; actual < standard → Favorable (for a cost).
  • The key discipline: always compare against the standard allowed for the output actually produced, never the original static budget.
Price/rate vs. quantity/efficiency (same shape every time)
Materials price = (AP − SP) × AQ purchased
Materials quantity = (AQ used − SQ allowed) × SP
Labor rate = (AR − SR) × AH
Labor efficiency = (AH − SH allowed) × SR
VOH spending = Actual VOH − (AH × std VOH rate)
VOH efficiency = (AH − SH allowed) × std VOH rate
  • Sign rule: a positive result (actual > standard) is Unfavorable; negative is Favorable. Report the amount as a positive number with an F/U label.
  • SQ / SH "allowed" = standard input per unit × actual units produced. This is the single most common place to slip.
  • Price variances multiply by the actual quantity; quantity/efficiency variances multiply by the standard price/rate.
VarianceCommon causes
Materials price FVolume discount, distressed seller, hard bargaining. (Buying higher-quality material raises price → unfavorable.)
Materials quantity UPoorly trained workers wasting material, inferior materials, faulty equipment.
Labor efficiency FSkilled, experienced workers; well-maintained equipment; good material quality.
Revenue vs. volume
Revenue variance = (Actual units × actual price) − (Budgeted units × budgeted price)
Net sales volume variance = (Actual units − budgeted units) × budgeted contribution margin per unit
  • The revenue variance (for a revenue center) captures the total top-line difference — both price and volume effects together.
  • The net sales volume variance isolates the volume effect and values it at the budgeted contribution margin per unit (not price, not full revenue).

Chapter 12

Capital Budgeting

  • Capital expenditures: investments of significant resources in projects to develop/introduce products, expand capacity, or change facilities.
  • Cost of capital: the average cost of funds from creditors and shareholders — often used as the discount rate.
  • Phases: initial/investment (the outlay) → operations (cash receipts & expenditures over the life) → divestment/end (salvage, recovery of working capital).
Payback
Even cash flows: Payback = Initial investment / annual cash inflow
Uneven: full years to recover + (unrecovered cost / next year's inflow)
  • A quick screening tool: how fast cash is recovered. Shorter = less risky.
  • Weaknesses: ignores the time value of money and ignores all cash flows after payback — so it doesn't measure overall profitability.
NPV
NPV = (PV of all cash inflows) − initial investment
Equal inflows → use the PV-of-annuity factor × annual inflow
Uneven inflows → discount each year with the PV-of-$1 factor, then sum
  • NPV decision rule: NPV ≥ 0 → the project earns at least the discount rate → acceptable. NPV < 0 → reject.
  • IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV = 0. Accept if IRR ≥ the cost of capital.
  • IRR shortcut for equal cash flows: compute the annuity factor = initial investment / annual inflow, then find the rate whose factor (for that number of periods) matches it in the table.
ARR
ARR = Average annual operating income / Average investment
Average investment = (Initial investment + Salvage value) / 2
  • ARR uses accrual net income (after depreciation), not cash flow, and it ignores the time value of money.
  • When a problem says "based on average investment," divide by (initial + salvage)/2 — not the full initial cost. Using the full initial investment is the classic wrong denominator.

Practice

Self-Check Questions

Fresh problems — different companies and numbers from the exam and the practice worksheet. Work each one on paper first, then reveal the worked answer. If you can do all eleven cleanly, you're in good shape.

Chapter 9 — Budgeting

Self-Check 1 · Production budget
Basswood Bikes expects to sell 12,000 units in July and 15,000 in August. It keeps ending finished-goods inventory equal to 30% of next month's sales. July 1 inventory is 3,600 units. Compute July's budgeted production.
Desired ending FG = 30% × 15,000 = 4,500 Production = 12,000 sales + 4,500 ending − 3,600 beginning = 12,900 units
Self-Check 2 · Merchandise purchases
Rivermark Retail budgets August cost of goods sold of $210,000. Beginning merchandise inventory is $26,000 and desired ending inventory is $34,000. Compute budgeted purchases.
Purchases = COGS + desired ending − beginning = 210,000 + 34,000 − 26,000 = $218,000
Self-Check 3 · Direct labor budget
Fernwood Furniture budgets production of 8,000 chairs. Each chair requires 0.75 direct-labor hours, plus 4 setup-hours per batch, with 50 batches scheduled. The wage rate is $19 per hour. Compute total budgeted direct-labor cost.
Hours = (8,000 × 0.75) + (50 × 4) = 6,000 + 200 = 6,200 hrs Cost = 6,200 × $19 = $117,800

Chapter 10 — Standard Costs & Variances

Self-Check 4 · Responsibility centers
The manager of Northgate Division controls the division's revenues and costs and is held accountable for the level of assets invested in the division, and is evaluated on return on investment. What type of responsibility center is Northgate?
Revenues + costs + invested assets, judged on ROI = Investment center
Self-Check 5 · Materials variances
Alderman Mfg has a standard of 3 pounds per unit at $5.00 per pound. It produced 2,000 units and purchased and used 6,300 pounds at $4.80 per pound. Compute the materials price and quantity variances (amount and F/U).
SQ allowed = 3 × 2,000 = 6,000 lbs Price = (4.80 − 5.00) × 6,300 = −1,260 → $1,260 F Quantity = (6,300 − 6,000) × 5.00 = +1,500 → $1,500 U Price $1,260 F · Quantity $1,500 U
Self-Check 6 · Labor variances
Coldwater Tools has a standard of 2.5 direct-labor hours per unit at $16.00 per hour. It produced 4,000 units and used 10,300 hours at $15.60 per hour. Compute the labor rate and efficiency variances (amount and F/U).
SH allowed = 2.5 × 4,000 = 10,000 hrs Rate = (15.60 − 16.00) × 10,300 = −4,120 → $4,120 F Efficiency = (10,300 − 10,000) × 16.00 = +4,800 → $4,800 U Rate $4,120 F · Efficiency $4,800 U
Self-Check 7 · Net sales volume variance
Brightleaf Co. budgeted sales of 10,000 units at $40 each, with a budgeted variable cost of $24 per unit. Actual sales were 9,300 units. Compute the net sales volume variance (amount and F/U).
Budgeted CM/unit = 40 − 24 = $16 = (9,300 − 10,000) × 16 = −700 × 16 = −11,200 = $11,200 U (sold fewer units than budgeted)

Chapter 12 — Capital Budgeting

Self-Check 8 · Payback (uneven)
Halcyon Co. invests $90,000 in a project with net cash inflows of Year 1 $30,000, Year 2 $36,000, Year 3 $40,000. Compute the payback period (two decimals).
After 2 years: 30,000 + 36,000 = 66,000 recovered Unrecovered = 90,000 − 66,000 = 24,000; Year 3 inflow = 40,000 = 2 + (24,000 / 40,000) = 2 + 0.60 = 2.60 years
Self-Check 9 · NPV (equal cash flows)
Kestrel Co. buys equipment for $120,000 that generates equal annual cost savings of $32,000 for 5 years. The discount rate is 10% (PV-of-annuity factor, 5 periods at 10% = 3.79079). Compute the NPV.
PV of savings = 32,000 × 3.79079 = 121,305.28 NPV = 121,305.28 − 120,000 = $1,305.28 (positive → accept)
Self-Check 10 · IRR (equal cash flows)
Dunmore Co. considers a project costing $72,000 with equal annual inflows of $19,000 for 5 years. Using the 5-period annuity factors (8% = 3.99271, 10% = 3.79079, 12% = 3.60478), the IRR is closest to which rate?
Factor = 72,000 / 19,000 = 3.7895 3.7895 matches the 10% factor (3.79079) almost exactly IRR ≈ 10%
Self-Check 11 · ARR on average investment
Vireo Co. considers a project costing $180,000 with an expected salvage value of $20,000. It generates average annual net income of $25,000. Compute the ARR based on average investment.
Average investment = (180,000 + 20,000) / 2 = 100,000 ARR = 25,000 / 100,000 = 25%
