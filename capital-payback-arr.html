Step 2

When the cash flows aren't level

payback with a partial final year

Project B — the cold-brew & pastry bar. It costs $115,000, also runs 4 years, and has a $15,000 salvage value. But a new bar takes time to build a following, so its net operating cash inflows ramp up over the four years rather than staying level:

Year 1$28,000
Year 2$34,000
Year 3$38,000
Year 4$42,000

With uneven inflows you can't just divide. First build the cumulative cash recovered at the end of each year. Then find the year in which the running total passes the $115,000 cost — that's where payback happens — and work out the fraction of that year needed.

Step 3

From cash flow to a rate of return

accounting rate of return, Project A

Payback tells Ramble how fast the cash returns, but not how profitable the project is. The accounting rate of return works from net income, not cash flow — and the operating cash inflow is not the same as net income. Your first job is to figure out what has to change to get from one to the other.

For Project A: cost $100,000, salvage $20,000, life 4 years, level operating cash inflow $32,000 per year. Ramble reports ARR two ways — once against the full initial investment, once against the average investment over the asset's life.

The $32,000 is a cash figure — not the same as net income. Put a number on the adjustment and build up the return.
